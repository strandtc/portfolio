Sizing and metering rack power

How to size a rack circuit under the continuous load rules, how A/B redundancy gets defeated in practice, and what stranded capacity costs.

Critical Power8 min read

The rack circuit is where the entire power chain either pays off or gets thrown away. You can build a beautiful 2N plant — two utility feeds, paralleled generators, N+1 UPS, redundant switchgear — and then have a technician plug both cords of a server into the same PDU, and none of the upstream investment matters for that server.

It is also where most of the capacity in a data center goes to die, quietly, in circuits provisioned for loads that never arrive.

Sizing the circuit

Start with the code, because the code is the reason the numbers look the way they do.

The NEC defines a continuous load in Article 100 as one where the maximum current is expected to continue for three hours or more. IT load is continuous. It does not turn off at lunch.

For continuous loads, 210.19(A)(1) requires branch-circuit conductors to have an ampacity of at least 125% of the continuous load, and 210.20(A) requires the overcurrent device to be rated at least 125% of the continuous load. Article 645, which covers information technology equipment, says the same thing in its own words: branch-circuit conductors supplying IT equipment must have an ampacity not less than 125% of the total connected load.

Dividing by 1.25 gives you the number everyone quotes: you get 80% of the breaker's rating. There is an exception where the assembly, including the overcurrent device, is listed for operation at 100% of its rating — those exist and they are expensive, and unless you specified one deliberately, assume you have the ordinary kind.

The arithmetic, for the circuits you will actually see:

usable kW = V × A × 0.8            (single phase)
usable kW = V × A × √3 × 0.8       (three phase)

120 V, 20 A, 1φ    →  120 × 20 × 0.8            =  1.9 kW
208 V, 30 A, 1φ    →  208 × 30 × 0.8            =  5.0 kW
208 V, 30 A, 3φ    →  208 × 30 × 1.732 × 0.8    =  8.6 kW
208 V, 60 A, 3φ    →  208 × 60 × 1.732 × 0.8    = 17.3 kW
415 V, 30 A, 3φ    →  415 × 30 × 1.732 × 0.8    = 17.2 kW
415 V, 60 A, 3φ    →  415 × 60 × 1.732 × 0.8    = 34.5 kW

Two notes on that table. These are volt-amps; modern server power supplies with active PFC run near unity power factor so kW and kVA are close, but the breaker responds to current, so size on VA. And the 415/240 V line is the reason high-density halls moved to it — you get the same kW down a 30 A whip that a 60 A whip delivers at 208 V, with half the copper and half the I²R loss.

The rule that gets violated most often: the 80% is not a safety margin you can borrow against. It is the code-required rating of the circuit. A rack that "only occasionally" pulls 95% of the breaker is not running with margin, it is running out of compliance, and the first cold-start inrush after a transfer will find out.

Single-corded, dual-corded, and how people defeat A/B

The premise of A/B distribution is that each rack gets two independent circuits from two independent paths, and either one can carry the whole rack alone.

Dual-corded equipment makes this work natively. Two power supplies, both energized, sharing the load roughly evenly. Lose one side and the surviving supply picks up the full load, usually without a blip.

Here is the constraint people miss, and it is the single most common error I have seen on a data center floor:

With A and B both live, each side carries about half the rack load. But each side must be able to carry all of it. So the usable capacity of an A/B pair is the usable capacity of one circuit, not two.

A pair of 208 V 30 A three-phase feeds gives you 8.6 kW of rack, not 17.2 kW. If you load that rack to 14 kW because "there are two 8.6 kW feeds," you have built something that works perfectly right up until you take one side down for UPS maintenance, at which point the survivor sees 14 kW on an 8.6 kW circuit and trips. You will have converted a planned maintenance window into an outage, and you will have done it to the racks you were most careful about.

Under normal operation each side should sit near or below 40% of its breaker rating. That feels wasteful. It is the price of the redundancy you already paid for upstream.

Other ways A/B gets quietly defeated:

Both cords into the same PDU. Sometimes carelessness, usually because one side ran out of outlets of the right type. Color-code the cords and the PDUs, red and blue, and audit it. Nothing sophisticated beats walking the aisle with a flashlight.

Single-corded equipment. Firewalls, some storage controllers, KVM gear, older switches. The usual fix is a rack-mount automatic transfer switch, which takes A and B and presents one output. That is a reasonable answer, with two caveats: the ATS is now itself a single point of failure for everything behind it, and its capacity limits how much single-corded gear you can hang on it. Keep single-corded loads inventoried, because they are the list of things that will actually drop.

Both power supplies on the same upstream branch. Rarer, harder to spot. The rack looks correct — two PDUs, two cords — but both PDU whips landed on the same panel, or worse, the same breaker pair, at the RPP. This is caught by the circuit schedule, if the circuit schedule is accurate. Keep it accurate.

Failing to test. If you have never actually opened the A side under load, you do not know that B carries the room. Do it on a schedule, in a window, with people on the floor.

Metered, monitored, switched

Rack PDU marketing terms are inconsistent between vendors, so define them by capability rather than by name:

CapabilityWhat it gives youWhat it is worth
BasicOutlets. Nothing else.Fine in a lab. Not in production.
Inlet meteredTotal current per PDU, per phase, local display and networkThe minimum. Tells you how loaded the circuit is.
Outlet meteredPer-outlet current and energyChargeback, capacity planning, finding the one server drawing double
SwitchedRemote on/off per outletRemote hard reboot, outlet lockout, commissioning sequencing
EnvironmentalTemperature and humidity probes at inlet and exhaustCheapest rack-level thermal data you can buy

If you buy one capability, buy inlet metering with per-phase current. If you can afford outlet metering, get it — it turns capacity planning from an estimating exercise into a measurement, and it is what lets you bill a colocation customer for actual consumption rather than for the size of the breaker.

If you are billing on it, care about accuracy. Rack PDU energy metering is commonly specified to IEC 62053-21 class 1, which is ±1% under reference conditions. Proper revenue metering standards — ANSI C12.20, or IEC 62053-22 class 0.5S — are tighter, and there is a real difference between a meter that is revenue accurate and one that is certified for revenue billing. Know which one you have before you put its output on an invoice.

Switched PDUs are wonderful and slightly dangerous. Remote power cycling saves a truck roll at 3 a.m. It also means an authenticated session, or a bad script, can drop a rack. Restrict the interface, segment its management network, and set outlet-level permissions so that whoever can reboot a server cannot reboot the storage array next to it.

Branch circuit monitoring upstream

Rack PDUs tell you what the rack draws. Branch circuit monitoring at the floor PDU or remote power panel tells you what each breaker in that panel draws, from current transformers on every pole.

You want both, because they answer different questions. The rack PDU tells you whether the rack is near its limit. The BCM tells you whether the panel is, whether your circuit schedule matches reality, and where the load actually landed. On a floor with hundreds of circuits, BCM is how you find the panel that is 85% loaded on one phase while its neighbor sits at 30%.

BCM data is also what makes phased build-out safe. When you can see real, measured loading per panel and per phase, you can add racks with confidence instead of adding them against nameplate arithmetic and a prayer.

Three-phase racks and phase balance

Three-phase rack PDUs deliver more kilowatts per whip, and that is why dense racks use them. The complication is that the load does not distribute itself.

On a 208 V three-phase rack PDU, outlets are grouped and fed across phase pairs. If you fill the outlets in physical order — top to bottom, as anyone naturally would — you can end up with most of the load on one pair. The consequence is not subtle: the breaker sees the current on the most loaded phase, so an unbalanced rack trips well below its nameplate capacity. A 17.3 kW three-phase circuit carrying 12 kW badly distributed can be sitting at the limit on one phase while the other two idle.

Practical handling:

  • Populate outlets so that consecutive servers land on different phase pairs. Vendors label the phase

grouping on the PDU; follow the labels, not the physical order.

  • Monitor per-phase current, not just total. Alarm on imbalance as well as on absolute current.
  • Rebalance when you decommission. Pulling ten servers out of a rack unbalances it as surely as

installing them.

  • On a 208/120 V wye system feeding nonlinear loads, watch neutral current. Triplen harmonics from

single-phase switch-mode supplies add in the neutral rather than cancel, and the neutral can carry more current than any phase conductor.

What stranded capacity costs you

Stranded capacity is power you built, paid for, and cannot sell. It shows up when circuits are provisioned against nameplate rather than measured draw.

The mechanism is simple. Someone reads the label on a dual-supply server, which states the rating of each supply, and budgets that number. Real steady-state draw is routinely a fraction of it. Multiply by forty servers in a rack and you have provisioned two or three times the power the rack will ever use. That capacity is now allocated in the capacity model, reserved at the panel, and unavailable to anyone else — while the UPS, the generator, the switchgear and the cooling that support it sit idle.

Every stranded kilowatt is a kilowatt of UPS modules, generator, chiller capacity and gear that you capitalized and cannot monetize. In a colocation business it is also a rack you cannot sell.

The fix is metering plus discipline:

  1. Provision against measured peak draw with a defined headroom factor, not against nameplate.
  2. Reclaim on a schedule. Run a report of circuits whose 95th-percentile draw is under, say, half of

allocation, and go take the allocation back.

  1. Track allocated versus measured as a ratio, per panel and per room. If it is above about 1.5, you

have a capacity planning problem that no amount of new construction will fix.

  1. Right-size the circuit to the rack's real density. Two 208 V 30 A three-phase feeds for a 4 kW rack

of network gear is a waste of two breaker positions and 8.6 kW of allocation.

The one to check first

Walk into any hall and pick the racks with the most expensive equipment in them. For each, add up what the A side would carry if B disappeared right now, and compare it to 80% of the A-side breaker rating.

If any rack fails that comparison, you do not have redundancy there. You have two circuits and a belief.
