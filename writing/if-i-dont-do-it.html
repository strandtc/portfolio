If I don't do it, nobody else will

The ownership stance that is correct at seven people, the point where it becomes the bottleneck, and how to tell you have crossed it.

Practice7 min read

At seven people there is no escalation path. There is no facilities group, no NOC, no compliance department, nobody to open a ticket with. If a cooling unit alarms at two in the morning, the list of people who might handle it is the same as the list of people who work there.

So "if I don't do it, nobody else will" is not a philosophy. At that size it is an accurate description of the org chart. I started at Liquid Web as employee number seven, and that sentence is how I decided what to work on for a long stretch. It was correct. Then at some point it stopped being correct, and the change was gradual enough that it is easy to miss entirely.

Why the instinct is right early

Three reasons, all structural rather than moral.

There is no redundancy, so the cost of anything not getting done is total. In a large organization, work that falls on the floor gets picked up eventually by somebody - late and badly, but picked up. In a seven-person company it simply does not happen. The network does not get built. The telemetry does not exist. Nobody is coming.

You end up with a model of the whole system. Building the power chain, the network and the facilities monitoring yourself means you understand how they interact, and that is knowledge you cannot buy and cannot really be taught. When something fails in a way that crosses three disciplines

  • a power event that shows up as a network symptom and gets diagnosed as a server problem - that model

is what finds it. The people who only ever owned one layer will still be arguing about whose fault it is.

Breadth beats depth at that size. Nobody needs a switchgear specialist at seven people. They need someone who will read the one-line, call the utility, learn what a maintenance bypass is for, and then go rack servers.

There is a fourth effect, which is that the first handful of people set what "we handle it" means in that company. That definition outlives them by years.

What it costs even when it is right

Be honest about the bill, because it comes due later and in full.

You do not document anything, because you are the documentation. You create single points of failure that are people rather than equipment, which is worse - you cannot buy a spare and you cannot test the failover. And you skip stages. Crawl, walk, run exists for a reason; when you can personally carry something, you build things that work only while you are carrying them, which is fine right up until the week you cannot.

None of that is an argument against the instinct at seven people. It is an argument for knowing what you are accumulating.

Where it turns

Somewhere well short of a hundred people the sentence stops being literally true. There are other people now. They will do it differently, more slowly, and worse the first three times. "Nobody else will do it" quietly becomes "nobody else will do it the way I would," which is a different sentence with a different correct answer.

The signal is not headcount. It is queueing. Work backing up behind one person, decisions waiting on one calendar, change windows scheduled around one individual's availability. That is a capacity problem, and the person creating it is usually the last to name it, because from the inside it feels like being busy and necessary.

What you observeAt seven people it meansAt two hundred it means
You are on the escalation for everythingCorrect, there is nobody elseYou never built the tier beneath you
You fixed it yourself in ten minutesEfficientYou deleted the week's only training opportunity
Nobody else understands the systemUnavoidable for nowA risk you own and have not written down
You are working most weekendsThe jobThe team is under-scoped, or you will not hand off
Someone did it wrong so you took it backReasonableThe reason nobody attempts it next time

Telling the difference

Three questions, in ascending order of how uncomfortable they are.

If I disappeared for two weeks, what stops? Not what would be harder - what actually stops. A long answer is not evidence of dedication. It is a list of design defects with your name on each one.

Am I the only person who can do this, or the only person who has done it? These feel identical from the inside and are completely different problems. The first is a skills gap and takes months of deliberate development. The second is a scheduling problem and takes one maintenance window with somebody standing next to you.

Am I doing this because it is urgent, or because doing it is more comfortable than teaching it? Doing is comfortable. You know how it goes and it will come out right. Teaching means watching someone do it worse than you would while you keep your hands in your pockets, and it is genuinely unpleasant. Most people who describe themselves as too busy to delegate are reporting this feeling accurately and labeling it wrong.

What has to change

Accept worse output for a while. Somebody else's eighty percent this month beats your hundred percent next quarter, because their eighty becomes ninety-five and your hundred stays a queue. That is arithmetic rather than generosity, and it is still hard to do the first several times.

Change the unit of work. From tasks to the things that make tasks happen without you: runbooks, on-call rotations, training, monitoring that pages the right group instead of the person who always answers. Building a support organization - hiring for it, training it, setting SLAs and then actually measuring whether customers got what they were promised - is a different craft from fixing things. Being excellent at fixing things does not make you good at it. It has to be learned on purpose.

Change what you measure yourself on. Not what you finished. What went correctly while you were not involved. That number is uncomfortable the first time you look at it honestly.

Deal with the identity part. For a lot of people who join a company early, being the one who handles it is a substantial part of how they understand themselves. Handing it over feels like being made unnecessary. It is not the same thing, but the feeling is real, and pretending otherwise is how you end up taking work back without being able to explain why.

What to keep

Do not throw the instinct away. It stays useful. What changes is the object.

In a large organization the most dangerous work is the work between teams - the problem spanning facilities, network and systems where every group can say with complete honesty that it is not theirs. Nobody's monitoring covers it. Nobody's runbook mentions it. Audit findings live there, and so do the outages that take longest to diagnose, because three groups each successfully proved it was not them.

That work still belongs to whoever notices it, and that part of the instinct does not expire.

What changes is the response. At seven people, noticing it and doing it are the same action. At four hundred and fifty, noticing it and giving it an owner is the action - and doing it yourself is how it stays unowned for another year.
