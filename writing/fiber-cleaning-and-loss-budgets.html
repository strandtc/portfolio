Fiber loss budgets and the connector nobody cleaned

How to build an optical link budget out of real numbers, and why every end face gets inspected before it gets mated.

Network8 min read

An optical link works when more light arrives at the receiver than the receiver needs to see. That is the whole thing. Everything else — connector counts, splice counts, cable length, dirt — is bookkeeping against that one inequality, and the bookkeeping is worth doing before you pull cable rather than after the link comes up dirty and nobody can explain why.

What the budget is made of

Two numbers come off the transceiver datasheet. Minimum launch power is the least light the transmitter is guaranteed to put into the fiber. Receiver sensitivity is the least light the receiver is guaranteed to work with. The difference between them is your entire budget, in dB.

Take a 10GBASE-LR module. Minimum launch is around −8.2 dBm and sensitivity is around −14.4 dBm, which gives you roughly 6 dB to spend on the cable plant. That is not a lot. It is also not a target — it is a ceiling, and you want to land meaningfully under it so the link still works after somebody adds a patch panel, after the connectors age, and after the fiber gets a little worse from being handled.

Two dB of margin is a reasonable thing to hold back. If your calculated loss plus your margin exceeds the budget, you do not have a link; you have an argument waiting to happen.

Where the loss comes from

There are three contributors: the fiber itself, every mated connector pair, and every splice.

TIA-568.3 gives design maxima. Those are worst-case allowances for acceptance testing, not predictions of what a competent installer will hand you.

ItemTIA-568.3 maximumWhat good work actually measures
Mated connector pair0.75 dB~0.3 dB
Splice (fusion or mechanical)0.3 dB~0.15 dB, single-mode fusion
Multimode fiber @ 850 nm3.5 dB/km~3 dB/km
Multimode fiber @ 1300 nm1.5 dB/km~1 dB/km
Single-mode, indoor cable1.0 dB/km—
Single-mode, outdoor cable0.5 dB/km~0.4–0.5 dB/km at 1310 nm

OS2 fiber to ITU-T G.652.D is specified at no more than 0.4 dB/km from 1310 to 1625 nm and no more than 0.3 dB/km at 1550 nm. If you are designing a metro span, use the fiber spec. If you are designing a building link, the connectors dominate and the glass barely matters.

Build the budget two ways. Design it against the standard maxima, so you know the link passes even if every termination is mediocre. Then estimate it against typical values, so you know what the OTDR trace should look like when it comes back. If the measured loss lands near the maximum rather than near the estimate, something is wrong even though the link "passed."

A worked budget

Eight kilometers of OS2 in duct, patched through a panel at each end, spliced from outside plant to indoor pigtails at both entrances, plus a cross-connect in the middle of one building.

Fiber        8.0 km  x 0.4 dB/km   =  3.20 dB
Splices      2       x 0.3 dB      =  0.60 dB   (standard max)
Connectors   6 pairs x 0.75 dB     =  4.50 dB   (standard max)
                                      -------
Design-max plant loss                  8.30 dB

Available budget (-8.2 dBm launch, -14.4 dBm sensitivity)
                                       6.20 dB

That design does not close. Not because the fiber is long, but because six mated pairs at the standard maximum eat more than the glass does. Take out the mid-span cross-connect and use typical rather than maximum connector loss and the same span lands near 4.5 dB, which works with margin — but only if the terminations are genuinely good, which brings us to the actual subject.

Connectors, not distance, are what kills short links. Every panel you add for tidiness costs you two mated pairs on the round trip. Design the patching, don't accumulate it.

Insertion loss and return loss are separate failures

Insertion loss is light that does not arrive. Return loss is light that comes back. They have different causes and different consequences, and a link can be fine on one and awful on the other.

Reflections come off the glass-air-glass discontinuity at a mated pair. Physical contact polishes reduce it by pressing the fibers together; angled polishes reduce it further by sending the reflection off into the cladding instead of straight back down the core. The commonly cited targets:

PolishTypical return loss target
PC~40 dB
UPC≥ 50 dB
APC (8° angle)≥ 60 dB

Return loss matters because reflected light gets back into the transmitter and destabilizes the laser, which shows up as jitter and bit errors rather than as a loss reading. It matters more on single-mode, more on analog and RF-over-fiber, and more on anything with a narrow-linewidth source. It is also why you never mate an APC connector to a UPC one: the 8° face won't seat against the flat one, you get an air gap, and both your insertion loss and your return loss go to pieces. Green boot means angled. Keep the colors honest in your patch cord bin.

What dirt actually does

A single-mode core is 9 µm across. A one-micron particle sitting on it blocks on the order of one percent of the light — call it 0.05 dB, which sounds harmless until you remember you had 6 dB to spend. A nine-micron speck, still invisible to the naked eye, can cover the core outright.

Worse, contamination is not static. Mate a dirty connector and you press the particle into both end faces. It can scratch. It can hold the ferrules apart, creating an air gap that adds loss and destroys return loss at the same time. Then you unmate, and the particle has transferred to the other connector, which you then plug in somewhere else. One dirty patch cord will happily contaminate a whole panel.

Vendors and test-equipment makers have been saying for years that end-face contamination is the single largest cause of optical link problems, and everyone who has spent a night chasing a link that came good after a swab believes them.

Inspection, and what the standard says

The standard is IEC 61300-3-35, currently in its third edition, published 2022. It defines zones on the end face and gives pass/fail criteria for scratches and for defects — particles, pits and debris — in each zone, with separate criteria for SM-PC, SM-UPC, SM-APC, multimode and multi-fiber connectors.

The zones run outward from the middle: Zone A is the core, Zone B the surrounding cladding, Zone C the adhesive region and Zone D the outer contact area of the ferrule. The core zone is the strictest, for obvious reasons. The 2022 edition dropped Zones C and D from the pass/fail criteria, on the reasoning that what matters is whether debris out there is loose enough to migrate inward — so you still inspect the contact area and still try to clean it, but embedded material that survives repeated cleaning is no longer a failure by itself.

The standard also sets requirements on the scope. A microscope used for this work needs a small field of view of at least 250 µm and has to resolve defects down to 2 µm across and scratches 3 µm wide. A hand-held optical loupe does not do that. Buy a video probe with the analysis software and a set of tips for the connector types you actually have — bulkhead adapters as well as patch cord tips, because half of what needs inspecting is the port, not the jumper.

And never look into a fiber, with a scope or without one, until you know the far end is dark. Infrared light gives you no blink reflex.

Cleaning without making it worse

The procedure has a name — inspect before you connect — and four steps, and the order is the whole point:

  1. Inspect the end face.
  2. If it is dirty, clean it.
  3. Inspect again to confirm it is clean.
  4. Only then mate it.

Step 3 is the one people skip, and it is the one that catches the times you made things worse.

For technique: dry first. A cassette cleaner or a one-click pen usually clears loose particulate, costs seconds, and leaves no residue. If dry does not do it — and it will not touch oils, fingerprints, or gel from an outside-plant cable — go wet-to-dry: a fiber-grade solvent on a lint-free wipe or swab, immediately followed by a dry pass across the same face so nothing is left to evaporate into a ring. Never leave solvent sitting on an end face.

Things that are not cleaning tools: canned air, which blows contamination around and can spit propellant onto the glass; your shirt; the alcohol prep pad from the first aid kit, which is the wrong purity and full of water; and re-used swabs. Bulkhead ports get cleaned too, with the right tip, and they get cleaned before you inspect the jumper you are about to insert, not after.

Put dust caps back on. Every time, on both the connector and the port, even for a minute. A cap is cheaper than a truck roll and dramatically cheaper than an outage.

The habit worth building is this: a scope and a cleaning kit live in every fiber tech's bag, and nobody mates a connector they have not looked at. It feels slow for about a week. Then it stops being slow and you notice you have stopped having mystery link errors.
