SNMP and what monitoring actually costs you

How SNMP works, why community strings refuse to die, and the running cost of a monitoring system nobody puts in the budget.

Systems9 min read

SNMP is a 1988 idea that runs most of the infrastructure telemetry on earth. It is under-secured, awkward to model, and has been declared obsolete roughly once every four years since the mid-nineties. It is also the only thing that a Cisco switch, an Arista switch, a rack PDU, a UPS, a CRAC unit and a Linux box will all speak without an agent you have to install and maintain. That is why it is still here.

How it works

There is an agent on the managed device and a manager that talks to it. The manager sends requests to UDP port 161; the device sends unsolicited notifications to the manager on UDP port

  1. UDP, not TCP, which is deliberate — the protocol was designed to still function when the

network is unhealthy, and a stateless datagram has a better chance of getting through a congested box than a handshake does.

The operations are few. GET fetches a named variable. GETNEXT fetches whatever comes after it in lexicographic order, which is how you walk a table without knowing its size. GETBULK, added in v2, returns many variables in one response so you are not paying a round trip per row. SET writes. TRAP and, later, INFORM are the device talking to you; INFORM is the one that gets acknowledged.

That is the whole protocol. Everything hard about SNMP is in the data model.

MIBs, OIDs, and the counter problem

Every variable has an object identifier — a dotted-decimal path through a global tree. The standard management branch is 1.3.6.1.2.1, and vendor-specific objects hang off 1.3.6.1.4.1.<enterprise number>. A MIB is a text file, written in a subset of ASN.1, that maps human-readable names onto those numbers and declares their types and access.

The MIB is documentation, not protocol. The device does not send you the MIB. If you do not have the right file loaded, 1.3.6.1.4.1.9.9.13.1.3.1.3.1004 is just a number, and half of operational SNMP work is chasing down the correct MIB revision for the exact firmware you are running.

The interface table, IF-MIB, is defined in RFC 2863 and is the single most-polled object in existence. It also contains the classic SNMP trap for the unwary: ifInOctets and ifOutOctets are 32-bit counters. RFC 2863 spells out what that means — the 32-bit octet counters wrap in about 57 minutes on 10 Mbps Ethernet and in roughly 12 minutes on a 45 Mbps T3. Run the same arithmetic forward:

2^32 bytes = 4.295 x 10^9 bytes = 34.36 gigabits

at    1 Gbps : 34.4 seconds to wrap
at   10 Gbps :  3.4 seconds to wrap
at  100 Gbps :  0.34 seconds to wrap

A five-minute poll interval against a 32-bit counter on a 10G interface is not measuring throughput. It is sampling a random number. The fix is the 64-bit high-capacity counters in the same MIB — ifHCInOctets and friends — which RFC 2863 notes will take just under five years to wrap on a 1 Tbps link. Use them. Verify your poller is actually using them, because plenty of legacy templates are not.

v1, v2c, v3, and why community strings will not die

v1RFC 1157 (STD 15)A cleartext community string
v2cRFC 1901 plus the v2 operationsThe same cleartext community string, with GETBULK
v3STD 62, RFCs 3411–3418User-based security model, RFC 3414; view-based access control, RFC 3415

A community string is a password sent in the clear in every packet, over UDP, with no replay protection. The read-only community on a shocking proportion of equipment is still public. There was a serious attempt to fix this in v2 with a party-based security model; it was too complicated, nobody implemented it, and v2c — "community-based v2", the security-free compromise — is what shipped and what stuck.

SNMPv3 does the job properly. USM gives you three security levels: noAuthNoPriv (identity only), authNoPriv (authenticated, still readable on the wire) and authPriv (authenticated and encrypted). VACM lets you scope a user to a subtree, so a monitoring account can read the interface table and nothing else.

So why is v2c everywhere? Because v3 is per-device user provisioning with per-device engine IDs and key localization, and that has to be automated to be survivable. Because embedded management cards in UPSs, PDUs and environmental gear ship with v3 implementations that are incomplete, buggy, or absent. Because on a management VLAN that is genuinely isolated, the marginal risk of v2c reads is low enough that it never reaches the top of anyone's list.

That reasoning is defensible right up until the management VLAN turns out not to be isolated. The minimum position I would defend: v3 with authPriv on anything that supports it competently; v2c read-only, never read-write, on an isolated management network, for everything else; and SNMP write access disabled globally unless you have a specific documented need for it.

Polling versus traps

Polling is a manager asking, on a schedule. It gives you a regular time series, it tells you when a device stops answering, and it costs you a request-response per object per interval, forever.

Traps are the device telling you. They are immediate and cheap, and they are UDP, so they can be silently lost. A device that is on fire may also fail to send the trap about being on fire.

Neither alone is a monitoring system. Poll for state and for anything you want to graph. Take traps for events you could never poll fast enough to catch — a power transfer, a breaker trip, a battery on discharge — and treat them as hints that trigger an immediate poll rather than as authoritative facts. The trap tells you to go look. The poll tells you what is true.

The lineage

Anyone who has run infrastructure for a while can date themselves by which of these they used in anger.

MRTG arrived in 1995: Tobias Oetiker's Perl script that polled two SNMP counters and drew a traffic graph. Its data store was the bottleneck, so Oetiker wrote RRDtool, released in July 1999 — a fixed-size round-robin database that consolidates old samples into coarser buckets so the file never grows. That single design decision defined the next fifteen years of infrastructure monitoring. Cacti, started by Ian Berry in 2001, put a web front end and a device model on top of RRDtool and made SNMP graphing something you could hand to someone else. I ran Cacti for years against network and facilities gear, and it did exactly what it said.

Alongside that lineage, Nagios began in 1996 solving a different problem: not graphing, but "is it up, and who do I wake." Check scripts, hosts, services, notifications, escalations. Between Cacti for trends and Nagios for state, most operations teams had a complete picture by about 2005, assembled from two tools that did not know about each other.

Prometheus broke the model rather than extending it. Conceived at SoundCloud in 2012 and publicly announced in January 2015, it joined the CNCF in 2016 and in August 2018 became its second graduated project, after Kubernetes. The changes that mattered: metrics carry dimensional labels instead of living at a fixed path, so http_requests_total{code="500", instance="web-3"} is one metric with dimensions rather than a thousand separate RRD files. Targets are discovered, not configured. And there is a query language, so alerting rules and dashboards are expressions over the same data rather than thresholds bolted onto a graph.

SNMP did not go away. It got a translation layer — an exporter that polls SNMP and presents the result as labeled metrics — because the switch, the PDU and the CRAC unit still only speak SNMP. The successors for network devices are streaming telemetry over gNMI, and configuration over NETCONF and RESTCONF, modelled in YANG and OpenConfig instead of SMI. They are genuinely better. They are also not supported by the eight-year-old PDU in cabinet 14.

What it actually costs

The software is free. The cost is elsewhere, and it is recurring.

Poll load, on the wrong side. Your poller is fine. The embedded management processor in a PDU or a UPS network card is a small microcontroller with other jobs, and hammering it with a walk of the entire tree every thirty seconds can make it slow, unresponsive, or — genuinely — reboot. Poll facilities gear gently, ask for specific OIDs rather than walking, and back off when a device starts timing out instead of retrying harder.

Cardinality. Modern label-based systems make it easy to create a metric per interface per device per queue per direction, and the storage and query cost scales with the product, not the sum. Deciding what not to collect is real engineering work and nobody schedules it.

Retention. Someone has to answer how long you keep raw resolution, what you downsample to, and what you keep for years for capacity planning and audit. Get it wrong in one direction and you cannot answer "was this happening in March." Get it wrong in the other and you are paying for storage of second-resolution data from a decommissioned rack.

MIB and template drift. Every firmware upgrade can move an OID, change a unit, or deprecate an object. Your dashboard keeps drawing a line. The line is now wrong, and nothing tells you.

Human attention. This is the big one. Every alert you add draws down a finite budget of on-call attention, and once that budget is overdrawn people stop reading alerts — including the ones that matter. A monitoring system with 400 alerts firing daily is worse than one with none, because the one with none does not give you false confidence.

Some rules I would hold to:

  • Poll with 64-bit counters. Check that you are, don't assume.
  • Never enable SNMP write unless something specific requires it, and then scope it with VACM.
  • Every new alert needs a documented action. If the action is "look at it," it is a dashboard,

not an alert.

  • Every alert that fires and gets ignored twice gets deleted or fixed. No third time.
  • Monitor the monitoring: something outside the system has to notice when the system stops

collecting. Silence is the most dangerous state a monitoring platform can be in.
