What a circuit breaker is actually doing

Overload versus short circuit versus ground fault, what every knob on a trip unit changes, and the 80 percent rule people get wrong.

Critical Power8 min read

A circuit breaker looks like one device with one job. It is closer to three devices sharing an enclosure, because the three things it protects against are not the same problem.

Overload is current above the conductor's rating on an otherwise healthy circuit. Nothing is broken. The damage is thermal, over minutes, so the response has to be slow and inverse - tolerant enough to ride out a motor start, not so tolerant that 200 percent of rating sits there for an hour.

Short circuit is a bolted fault, current limited only by system impedance. Tens of thousands of amps in the first half cycle, damage by magnetic force and arc energy, units of cycles rather than minutes. The response has to be as fast as the mechanism can move.

Ground fault is current leaving through equipment grounding conductors, conduit or building steel. The dangerous case is an arcing ground fault: a few hundred amps, below instantaneous pickup and far below long-time pickup on a large feeder. Phase overcurrent logic never sees it. So ground fault protection is a separate element that senses the residual sum of the phases rather than the magnitude of any one.

One response cannot cover all three. That is the whole architecture of a trip unit.

What actually opens the contacts

Thermal-magnetic breakers put two mechanisms in one case. Overload is handled by a bimetallic strip - two metals with different expansion rates bonded together, heated by load current, deflecting until it releases the trip latch. It is a thermal analogue of conductor heating, and it gives an inverse curve for free. Short circuit is handled by a magnetic armature, steel pulled by the field around the current path, tripping the latch with no thermal lag. Cheap, robust, no control power. Two weaknesses: the bimetal responds to ambient as well as load, so the same breaker trips early in a hot enclosure and late in a cold room, and nothing is adjustable except, on larger frames, instantaneous pickup.

Purely magnetic breakers, usually sold as hydraulic-magnetic, have no bimetal. A coil surrounds a spring-loaded core in a sealed tube of silicone fluid. Current pulls the core toward the pole piece, the fluid governs how fast it travels, and the delay is inversely proportional to current. Nothing in the mechanism is a temperature sensor, so the trip point does not drift with ambient and the breaker does not have to cool before it resets. Hence DC plants, telecom and hot equipment enclosures rather than building distribution.

Electronic trip units use current transformers or Rogowski coils on each pole, a microprocessor and an actuator. They measure true RMS, and every protection function becomes a number you can change. That is the point of them. Coordination is an argument about which device opens first, and you cannot win that argument with two fixed curves - an adjustable trip unit is what makes selective coordination achievable at all.

Ranges are from Roybal's IEEE survey of the three classes; catalogs have crept up since. An ICCB is a molded case breaker that grew up - there is no ANSI, NEMA or UL definition of the term, so ratings vary between manufacturers more than you would like. A low-voltage power circuit breaker is a different animal: built to be maintained, tested inside its enclosure rather than in free air, and available with no instantaneous element at all. That last point is the architectural one. Coordinating in the fault region needs an upstream device that will deliberately wait, and waiting needs short-time withstand. UL 1066 and ANSI C37.50 test power breakers by applying rated short-time current for 30 cycles, pausing 15 seconds, then applying it again. A molded case breaker is neither built nor tested for that.

The trip curve, knob by knob

Trip units are named by the functions they carry, which is where LSIG comes from: long-time, short-time, instantaneous, ground fault. The curve is log-log, current across and clearing time up. Two references matter: In, the sensor rating, and Ir, long-time pickup, set as a fraction of In. Short-time and ground settings are multiples of Ir, so moving long-time pickup drags the rest of the curve sideways with it.

Long-time pickup is where the curve's left edge sits, and it should track conductor ampacity. Turn it down and the overload region slides left. It is not a precise threshold - a typical unit trips somewhere between 1.05 and 1.20 times Ir, and the plotted band shows that.

Long-time delay sets how long an overload is tolerated. It is specified as a time at a reference multiple of pickup, commonly 6 × Ir, because that is roughly a motor's starting current. Turn it up and the whole inverse region lifts vertically; pickup does not move. Long time is normally I²t, so quadrupling current cuts the time by sixteen.

Short-time pickup is where the curve steps in from the long inverse slope to a much shorter delay, set as a multiple of Ir over a range like 1.5 to 10. Move it right and the step moves right. Short-time delay is how long the breaker holds that current; move it up and the shelf rises. This is the setting that buys selectivity, and the same setting raises incident energy. That trade never goes away.

I²t in or out changes the shape of the shelf. Out, the short-time region is definite time - flat at the delay you set, whatever the current. In, it gains an inverse ramp: below a fixed current, commonly 8 × Ir, the delay stretches longer at lower current and slopes down onto the flat portion above. Use I²t in when the downstream device has a sloped curve of its own, a fuse or a thermal-magnetic breaker. Use it out when you want maximum predictable delay against another electronic trip unit.

Instantaneous is a vertical line with no intentional delay. Many units let you switch it off, which is how an upstream breaker is made to wait. Switching it off does not remove it. Molded case and insulated case breakers carry a non-defeatable instantaneous override protecting contacts from currents they cannot hold, typically fixed around 10 to 13 times frame rating on an MCCB and higher on an ICCB. Worse, trip units generally detect instantaneous on peak, and peak depends on circuit X/R. Roybal works the case: a breaker whose 65 kA short-delay rating was established on a symmetrical wave has an override near 91,650 A peak, but 50 kA RMS available at an X/R of 6.6 produces a first half cycle of 115 kA. The override fires and the short-time delay you were relying on never happens. Check the override in peak amps against the real X/R, not the RMS available fault current.

Ground fault senses the residual sum of the phases plus neutral, or uses a zero-sequence CT, with its own pickup and delay. It is equipment protection. Nothing about it is a GFCI.

Racking a breaker out

Drawout construction is why a maintainable electrical plant is possible. The breaker rides on rails in its own compartment and moves between four defined positions with nothing disconnected and the assembly still live:

  • Connected - primary and secondary disconnects engaged, carrying load.
  • Test - primaries disengaged, control circuits still live. Close it, trip it, prove the trip unit and interlocks with no load current near it.
  • Disconnected - everything disengaged, breaker still in the cell.
  • Withdrawn - off the rails, on a lift truck.

Only the load fed by that one breaker is affected. On a 2N plant this is what concurrent maintainability means in practice: rack out the A side, work on it, rack it back, prove it in test, close it.

The hazard is that racking drives moving primary contacts against energized bus. NFPA 70E's task table treats insertion or removal of breakers from cubicles, doors open or closed, as a task where an arc flash is likely. Remote racking - a motorized tool driving the mechanism from a cable, operator outside the arc flash boundary - is the reasonable answer, and on a 480 V lineup it can turn a high-energy task into a routine one. Do not treat racking as low risk because the door is shut.

Eighty percent, one hundred percent, and the arithmetic

NEC Article 100 defines a continuous load as one where the maximum current is expected to continue for three hours or more. IT load is continuous by definition.

The rule reads the same in three places - 210.20(A) for branch circuits, 215.3 for feeders, 230.42(A)(1) for service conductors: the rating of the overcurrent device shall not be less than the noncontinuous load plus 125 percent of the continuous load.

Note the direction. The Code sizes the device from the load. The "80 percent rule" is that arithmetic inverted, because 1 / 1.25 = 0.8, and it is a rule of thumb rather than code language. The distinction matters the moment someone hands you an existing breaker and asks what you may hang on it.

Eighty percent is also not a rating. UL 489 requires every molded case breaker to carry 100 percent of rating continuously without tripping, tested in open air in a chamber held at 40 °C. Put it in a can and the heat has nowhere to go. The 125 percent multiplier is the margin for that.

A 100 percent rated breaker has passed extra UL 489 testing: thermal testing inside an enclosure of specified minimum size, run to temperature stabilization without tripping, with limits on terminal temperature rise. Where terminal temperature exceeds 50 °C but stays under 60 °C, the listing requires 90 °C insulated wire sized at the 75 °C ampacity. Those conditions are printed on the breaker, and they read like this:

SUITABLE FOR CONTINUOUS OPERATION AT 100 PERCENT OF RATING ONLY IF
USED IN A CUBICLE SPACE 520 BY 420 BY 200 mm AND WHEN USED WITH 90C
WIRE. THE WIRE SIZE SHALL BE BASED ON THE AMPACITY OF 75C RATED WIRE

Three things get missed. The exception is written on the assembly, so a 100 percent rated breaker in a panelboard that is not listed for 100 percent operation does not earn it. The enclosure dimensions and ventilation are requirements. And the conductors must be 90 °C insulation at 75 °C ampacity with connectors to match. Power circuit breakers sidestep all of this - UL 1066 and ANSI C37.50 require them fully rated in the enclosure, so they are listed for 100 percent as standard and may use 75 °C cable.

On a feeder the difference is real money:

Noncontinuous 150 A, continuous 600 A
  Standard:   150 + (1.25 x 600) = 900 A -> 1000 A frame, 3 x 350 kcmil/phase
  100% rated: 150 + 600          = 750 A ->  800 A frame, 3 x 250 kcmil/phase

At the rack the same arithmetic runs the other way, and this is where people fumble:

208 V 1-phase, 20 A, continuous
  20 / 1.25 = 16 A -> 16 x 208 = 3,328 VA usable  (not 4,160)
208 V 3-phase, 30 A, continuous
  30 x 208 x 1.732 = 10,810 VA -> x 0.8 = 8,648 VA usable

The failure modes: capacity-planning the full handle rating, when a 20 A circuit is a 16 A circuit for IT load. Applying 80 percent twice, because the PDU vendor already published 16 A for that 20 A strip. Assuming a 100 percent rated breaker fixes it, when rack-level branch devices generally are not listed for it and neither is the assembly. And forgetting that the same 125 percent lands on the conductor too, with ampacity correction and adjustment factors multiplying on top - then that the answer rarely lands on one of 240.6's standard ratings, so you round up and re-check the conductor.

The ratings you cannot fudge

Everything above is whether the breaker trips correctly. Interrupting rating is whether it survives doing so.

NEC 110.9 requires equipment intended to interrupt fault current to have an interrupting rating at nominal circuit voltage not less than the current available at its line terminals. It is voltage-specific: the same frame carries different ratings at 240, 480 and 600 V, and the number on the front of the catalog is usually the 240 V one. 110.10 is the companion - devices, impedances and equipment short-circuit current ratings coordinated so a fault clears without extensive damage. Interrupting rating is the breaker; SCCR is the assembly around it. Watch test power factor too: molded case breakers above 20 kA are tested at 0.15 to 0.20, power breakers at 0.15 maximum, and a higher calculated X/R than the device was tested at derates the published rating.

Series ratings let a lower-rated downstream breaker sit where available fault current exceeds its own interrupting rating, because a UL-tested combination with a specific fully rated upstream device shares the interruption. Only tested combinations count; you cannot reason your way to one, and NEC 110.22 requires the combination field-marked on the equipment. 240.86(C) rules them out where motors connect between the two devices and the sum of motor full-load currents exceeds 1 percent of the lower device's interrupting rating - for 14 kA branch breakers that is 140 A of motor load, about 25 hp. They are essentially a molded-case tool, and they are the opposite of coordination: under high fault conditions both devices may open.

Current-limiting devices reduce fault current to substantially less than what would flow if the device were replaced by a solid conductor of comparable impedance. UL 489 requires a current-limiting breaker to hold let-through I²t below that of a half-cycle wave of the available symmetrical current, and to be marked "Current Limiting" with its peak and I²t let-through values. Current-limiting fuses do it by clearing inside the first half cycle. Both protect downstream equipment whose SCCR you cannot raise, and both cut incident energy. The fuse trade is the usual one: single-phasing, replacement stock, and someone in front of energized gear with a fuse puller.

Three things before you sign off on a lineup. Read the whole label rather than the handle - interrupting rating at your voltage, continuous rating conditions, enclosure dimensions, wire temperature. Record the as-set trip values inside the cubicle door with a date, because trip units get swapped and settings default. And confirm the instantaneous override in peak amps against the real X/R at that bus, because that one number decides whether the short-time delay in your coordination study exists in the field or only on paper.
