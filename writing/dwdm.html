How DWDM turns one fiber pair into dozens of circuits

Why you multiplex wavelengths, how the ITU grid works, what transponders and amplifiers do, and what a metro ring is really made of.

Network8 min read

You have one pair of strands between two buildings and you need more than one circuit on it. That is the entire problem wavelength division multiplexing solves. You put several optical carriers at different wavelengths onto the same glass, they don't interfere with each other in any way that matters, and you separate them again at the far end with a passive filter.

Why you multiplex instead of buying more fiber

Sometimes you shouldn't. If you own conduit and the route is short, more strands are cheap. But on a leased or IRU route the strand count is fixed by a contract you signed years ago, and the cost of adding strands is a negotiation, not a purchase. Multiplexing converts a contract problem into an equipment problem, and equipment problems you can solve with a purchase order.

The second reason is diversity. Every additional pair you light on the same route is another circuit that dies in the same backhoe strike. Once you accept that a route is a single failure domain, there is no engineering reason to spend strands on it — you spend strands on getting a second route instead, and you multiplex both.

When I built the Lansing network the constraint was exactly this. Strands on a route were expensive and finite; a DWDM ring to the local ILEC let one pair carry every service we needed to hand off, and let us add services later without going back to the fiber owner.

The ITU grid

Channels have to land somewhere predictable or nothing from two vendors will ever work together, so the ITU publishes the grid.

ITU-T G.694.1 defines the DWDM frequency grid. It is specified in frequency rather than wavelength, anchored at 193.1 THz, with nominal central frequencies at 193.1 + n × the channel spacing. The defined fixed spacings are 12.5, 25, 50 and 100 GHz. G.694.1 was first published in 2002, gained the flexible-grid extension in 2012, and was last revised in October 2020. The flexible grid places centers on a 6.25 GHz granularity and allocates slot widths in multiples of 12.5 GHz, which is what lets a modern coherent carrier occupy however much spectrum it actually needs instead of being forced into a fixed 50 GHz box.

Most of this happens in the C-band, roughly 1530 to 1565 nm, which is about 4.4 THz wide. At 100 GHz spacing that is somewhere around 44–48 usable channels; at 50 GHz you roughly double it. The C-band is where everything lives for one reason: it is where erbium amplifiers work.

The ITU band plan around it, for context:

CWDM and DWDM

CWDM — coarse WDM, ITU-T G.694.2 — uses a fixed wavelength grid with 20 nm channel spacing from 1271 to 1611 nm, giving 18 nominal channels. That spacing is enormous compared to DWDM, and that is the point: 20 nm of separation means the transmitter can drift with temperature and still stay in its channel, so you can use an uncooled laser and a cheap passive filter.

The tradeoff is that CWDM channels are spread across the whole spectrum, most of them nowhere near the EDFA gain window. You generally cannot amplify a CWDM system, which caps you at whatever passive budget the optics have — realistically tens of kilometers.

For a campus, a couple of buildings across town, or a handful of 10G circuits, CWDM is the right answer and people over-engineer past it constantly. Once you need amplification, tunability, or more than 18 channels, you are in DWDM whether you wanted to be or not.

Transponders, muxponders, and alien wavelengths

Your router has a grey optic in it — 1310 nm, no particular channel, no relationship to the grid. Something has to convert that to a grid wavelength.

A transponder does exactly that and nothing else: grey client in on one side, one specific ITU channel out on the line side. One client, one wavelength.

A muxponder aggregates. Several clients in — say ten 10G ports — one line wavelength out at 100G or 200G. The economics are obvious: line-side optics and grid channels are the scarce, expensive resources, and a muxponder lets you fill one properly instead of burning ten channels on ten underused 10G circuits.

An alien wavelength is a carrier generated by someone else's equipment that transits your line system, or yours transiting theirs. The transponder function moves into the router — a coherent pluggable such as a 400ZR or OpenZR+ QSFP-DD sitting directly in a router port, tuned to a grid channel, launched into a mux you did not buy and terminated in a receiver the line-system owner never sees.

That is very attractive commercially and it moves the engineering burden onto you. The line system owner is no longer guaranteeing your circuit works, because they cannot see inside it. You now own the optical performance calculation: launch power into their mux, per-channel power management, OSNR at the far end, and whatever their amplifiers do to your carrier that they never characterized. Before you commit to alien waves, get written agreement on launch power, channel plan, and who troubleshoots what.

Amplification, dispersion, and what actually limits reach

Two things stop the signal.

Loss you fix with amplification. An EDFA — erbium-doped fiber amplifier — is a length of erbium-doped fiber pumped with a laser, which amplifies everything in the C-band at once, optically, without demultiplexing or regenerating anything. That "all channels at once" property is what makes DWDM economical: one amplifier serves the whole comb. L-band EDFAs exist and extend the same trick into 1565–1625 nm when you run out of C.

Amplifier spans are commonly engineered around 80 km, which is roughly what 0.2 dB/km of fiber and a practical amplifier gain budget produce. The thing that actually runs out is not power but OSNR — optical signal-to-noise ratio. Every amplifier adds noise as well as gain, so a chain of amplifiers grows the signal and the noise floor together. Cascade enough of them and you have plenty of power and an unusable signal.

Chromatic dispersion is the other limit. Different wavelengths travel at slightly different speeds in glass, so a pulse spreads as it propagates. On standard G.652 fiber that is about +17 ps/nm·km at 1550 nm, rising to roughly 20 ps/nm·km out in the L-band. Dispersion accumulates linearly with distance and hurts more as the symbol rate goes up, which is why a 10G direct-detect wave will run happily over a span that a 40G one won't.

Older systems fixed this with dispersion compensating fiber — a spool of fiber with the opposite dispersion sign, inserted at amplifier sites, which costs you loss and latency to buy back pulse shape. Coherent systems mostly don't bother. A coherent receiver recovers amplitude and phase and undoes accumulated dispersion in the DSP, which is why the whole DCF product category quietly disappeared from new builds.

What a metro ring actually looks like

Strip the marketing off and a metro DWDM ring is not complicated.

At each site there is a chassis holding transponders or muxponders on the client side, a mux/demux passive on the line side, and typically an amplifier — a booster on the outbound direction, a preamp on the inbound. Between sites there are two fibers, east and west, one per direction. An optical supervisory channel runs outside the amplified band carrying management traffic between shelves, so you can still reach a node whose traffic-carrying channels are down.

Sites that only add and drop a couple of channels use a fixed filter. Sites that need to steer channels use a ROADM — a reconfigurable optical add/drop multiplexer, built around wavelength-selective switches — which lets you route an individual wavelength through, or drop it locally, without touching a patch panel.

Protection is where the ring earns its name. Traffic goes both ways around it. Cut one segment and each wavelength still has a path in the other direction. You can do that at the optical layer with an optical protection switch that flips the whole carrier, or at the client layer by handing two paths to the router and letting the routing protocol decide. The optical version is faster; the client version is more visible and easier to reason about at three in the morning. Pick one deliberately, and make sure you have not accidentally built a "ring" whose two halves share a conduit for two blocks downtown, because then you have an expensive line, not a ring.

The number worth writing on the front of the shelf is the per-span loss you measured at turn-up. Everything you will ever argue about with the fiber provider is a delta from that number.
