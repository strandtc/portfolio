Aisle containment and the air you are already wasting

Bypass and recirculation are the real problem, containment is one answer, and delta-T is how you tell whether any of it worked.

Facilities9 min read

A cooling unit does not cool a room. It moves a volume of air at a temperature, and whether that air does useful work depends entirely on whether it passes through a server on the way back. Most of the money wasted on data center cooling is spent moving air that never touches a heat load. Containment is the most direct way to stop.

The enemy is bypass and recirculation

Hot aisle and cold aisle arrangement - cabinets facing each other across a shared cold aisle, backs to a shared hot aisle - is credited to Robert F. Sullivan, then at IBM, in the early 1990s. It was a large improvement on rows all facing the same way, where each row inhaled the previous row's exhaust. It also does not, by itself, keep the two air streams apart. It just makes them adjacent, and two failures follow.

Bypass airflow is conditioned supply air that returns to the cooling unit without passing through any IT equipment. It goes around the end of a row, over the top of the cabinets, through an unsealed cable cutout in the raised floor, through a perforated tile in a hot aisle, or through an empty rack unit where nobody fitted a blank. It costs you fan energy, it costs you cooling capacity, and it lowers your return temperature, which makes the cooling unit less effective at exactly the moment you need it.

Recirculation is hot exhaust that finds its way back to an equipment intake - over the top of a short cabinet, around the end of a row, or through the gaps in a partly populated rack. Recirculation is what produces hot spots. The room can sit comfortably within setpoint on average while one server inhales its neighbor's exhaust at 40 °C.

Everything below is aimed at those two things. Containment is a means, not the goal.

Containment approaches

ApproachWhat it doesTrade-offs
Cold aisle containmentRoof and end doors over the cold aisle; the rest of the room becomes hot returnCheap to retrofit, suits raised-floor supply; the room runs hot, which reduces ride-through if cooling stops
Hot aisle containmentEncloses the hot aisle and returns it to a ceiling plenum or the unitsRoom stays at supply temperature - comfortable, and a thermal buffer; needs a return path
Vertical exhaust duct (chimney cabinets)Ducts each cabinet's exhaust into the ceiling plenumPer-cabinet, no aisle work; depends on cabinet sealing, restricts airflow if undersized
Curtains and strip doorsPartial separation, cheapBetter than nothing; degrades as people leave them tied back

The choice between hot and cold aisle containment is less technical than it looks. Hot aisle is the better engineering answer in most new builds: the occupied room sits at supply temperature, so working in it is comfortable and there is a large volume of cool air acting as a buffer if cooling stops. Cold aisle containment is usually the better retrofit, because it installs over an existing raised-floor room without touching the ceiling.

What matters more than the choice is completeness. A contained aisle with a gap is a contained aisle with a leak path, and air will find it before you do.

The unglamorous parts

Containment fails on the details, and the details cost almost nothing relative to the panels.

Blanking panels in every empty rack unit. An open U in the middle of a populated cabinet is a direct short circuit from hot aisle to cold aisle, right at intake height. Cheapest airflow intervention available, most commonly skipped, usually because nobody owns the job after the racks are handed over.

Floor grommets on every cable cutout, and sealing under and between cabinets. In a raised floor, every unsealed opening that is not a perforated tile in a cold aisle is bypass. Cutouts behind cabinets are the worst offenders, because that air comes up into the hot aisle and goes straight home.

Filler panels between cabinets. Frames are rarely perfectly adjacent, and a two-inch gap between two of them is a leak path at every height.

Perforated tile placement. Tiles belong in cold aisles, in proportion to the load of the cabinets they serve. A perforated tile in a hot aisle, or under a cabinet, is a design error somebody installed on purpose to fix a hot spot and never removed.

Equipment that breathes sideways. Network switches, mostly. Side-to-side airflow in a front-to-back world creates a recirculation loop inside the cabinet. Use ducted airflow kits, or place them where the intake sees cold air.

None of that is interesting work. All of it returns more per dollar than the containment system it supports.

Delta-T is the metric that tells you the truth

Server manufacturers design equipment to raise the air temperature by a known amount as it passes through. That rise is set by the hardware and the fan speed, not by you. Which gives you a very useful diagnostic: if the temperature rise the cooling units see is much smaller than the rise the IT equipment produces, the difference is air that went around the servers.

That relationship is formalized as the Return Temperature Index, introduced by Magnus Herrlin:

RTI = (T_return - T_supply) / (delta-T across the IT equipment) x 100%

RTI = 100%   ideal: everything the units deliver passes through the load
RTI < 100%   net bypass air
RTI > 100%   net recirculation

Its companion is the Rack Cooling Index, also Herrlin's, which measures how well intake temperatures across the room comply with the ASHRAE recommended and allowable limits. RCI is expressed as a percentage with 100 % as the ideal - no intakes outside the recommended range at the high end, none at the low end.

The two together answer different questions. RCI asks whether the equipment is being cooled properly. RTI asks whether you are spending too much to do it. You can have a perfect RCI and an awful RTI, which is the normal condition of a room that solved its hot spots by adding cooling units.

In practice, a return temperature close to the supply temperature at your CRAC or CRAH units is the clearest single indicator of a bypass problem. It is also a measurement you already have, on equipment you already own, and almost nobody looks at it.

Raising supply temperature

The reason to fix airflow is not tidiness. It is that once the two air streams are genuinely separated, you can raise the supply temperature - and supply temperature is what determines how many hours a year you can run on economizers, how warm you can run the chilled water, and how efficiently the plant operates.

ASHRAE's TC 9.9 thermal guidelines give a recommended envelope of 18 to 27 °C (64.4 to 80.6 °F) at the equipment intake, with humidity between a −9 °C and 15 °C dew point and no more than 60 % relative humidity. The allowable classes are wider, and they are equipment classes rather than facility targets:

ClassAllowable dry bulbTypical equipment
A115 to 32 °CEnterprise servers, storage
A210 to 35 °CVolume servers
A35 to 40 °CExtended-temperature equipment
A45 to 45 °CWidest tolerance
H1up to 25 °C allowable, 18 to 22 °C recommendedHigh-density air-cooled equipment

H1 is the one that surprises people. Added in the fifth edition of the guidelines in 2021, it goes the other way: high-density air-cooled equipment, the sort now filling AI and HPC racks, packs so much power behind a fixed amount of airflow that it needs colder and more tightly controlled inlet air, not warmer. If you are planning for that load, re-examine the raise-the-setpoint argument for those rows.

For conventional load the argument holds, with one constraint. Every degree of supply temperature containment buys you is a degree you did not buy with mechanical cooling - but not indefinitely. Server fans ramp with inlet temperature, and past some point the IT fan power you add exceeds the plant power you save. There is an optimum, specific to your equipment mix, and you find it by walking the setpoint up slowly while watching total facility power and intake temperatures. Not by picking a number from a conference talk.

The fire suppression argument

Containment changes the room in ways the fire protection design assumed would not happen, and this is not a detail you sort out after commissioning.

The problems are concrete. Containment roofs and doors obstruct sprinkler spray patterns and block smoke from reaching ceiling-mounted detection. A contained hot aisle runs hot enough that detection and suppression components inside it need to be rated for that temperature. A gaseous system designed for a room volume behaves differently when part of that volume is partitioned off with high airflow through it.

NFPA 75, the standard for fire protection of information technology equipment - current edition 2024 - addresses this directly: where aisle containment is installed, the existing detection and suppression systems have to be evaluated, modified as necessary, and tested. One specific change is worth knowing, because it invalidates a common older design. Drop-away containment panels held by fusible links, which release on heat, are no longer acceptable on their own. Removal of the obstruction has to be initiated by smoke detection, which means the containment has to be electrically coupled to the detection system and has to fail in the open direction.

The practical consequence: if you are retrofitting containment into an existing room, the fire protection engineer and the authority having jurisdiction are involved from the first sketch, not at the end. Retrofitting sprinkler heads into a contained aisle after the panels are up costs far more than designing for it.

Walking the room

If you want to know where you stand before spending anything, walk it with a thermal camera, a tile flow hood if you have one, and a notebook:

  • Read return temperature at every cooling unit and compare it to supply. If the difference is much

smaller than the rise across your servers, you have bypass, and you can quantify roughly how much.

  • Count empty rack units without blanks. That number is your first work order.
  • Find every floor cutout without a grommet, including the ones under cabinets.
  • Find every perforated tile that is not in a cold aisle serving a load.
  • Measure intake temperature at the top front of the end cabinet in each row. Recirculation shows up

there first.

  • Count how many cooling units are running, and how many you would need if the air went where it was

supposed to.

Most rooms find enough in that walk to defer a cooling unit purchase. Containment is the step after, not instead of.
