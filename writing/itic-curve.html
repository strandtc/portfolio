What the ITIC curve actually claims about clean power

The real breakpoints on the ITI power quality curve, what its three regions do and do not promise, and where server ride-through actually comes from.

Critical Power7 min read

Ask ten people what the ITIC curve says and you get ten versions, most wrong the same way: they read the area under the envelope as where equipment breaks. It is not. It is where equipment is explicitly not expected to keep working and explicitly not expected to be damaged. Two different claims, and the gap between them is the point.

What the curve actually says

The ITI (CBEMA) Curve Application Note comes from the Information Technology Industry Council — formerly CBEMA, the Computer & Business Equipment Manufacturers Association, whose 1970s curve described mainframe-era tolerance. ITI published the wider version in 1996 and revised it in October 2000. Plot and text are one document; they "are not to be considered separately from each other," and nearly everyone reproduces the plot and drops the text — which is where the misreadings start.

RMS voltage against event duration, log time, three regions. Inside is an envelope that "typically can be tolerated (no interruption in function) by most Information Technology Equipment." Below and left is the no damage region: sags and dropouts worse than the envelope allows, plus steady voltage under the lower limit. Normal function is not expected there, but no damage should result. Above is the prohibited region, any surge or swell over the upper limit, where damage may result.

A sag in the no-damage region has hurt nothing. It just dropped your load. Seven conditions make up the envelope:

ConditionLimitDuration
Steady-state tolerance90% to 110% of nominalIndefinite
Voltage sag80% of nominalUp to 10 s
Voltage sag70% of nominalUp to 0.5 s
Dropout0%, complete interruptionUp to 20 ms
Line voltage swell120% RMSUp to 0.5 s
Low-frequency decaying ringwave140% of 60 Hz peak at 200 Hz, linearly to 200% at 5 kHzDecays within the half cycle
High-frequency impulse and ringwaveANSI/IEEE C62.41-1991 waveshapes, 80 joule minimum immunityTransient

The lower boundary is a staircase, not a slope: zero volts out to 20 ms, a step to 70% out to 0.5 s, 80% out to 10 s, then 90% indefinitely. There is no 50% breakpoint. If you have seen "50% for 200 ms" attached to the ITI curve, that is SEMI F47 leaking in.

What it is not

The scope statement gets skipped. The curve applies to 120 V nominal from 120 V, 208Y/120 V and 120/240 V 60 Hz systems; other voltages and frequencies "are not specifically considered." It is, in its own words, "not intended to serve as a design specification for products or AC distribution systems." Nothing certifies to it, it promises nothing about your specific server, and it says nothing about frequency.

Its three neighbors use different verbs. IEEE 1159 classifies: a sag is 0.1 to 0.9 pu for half a cycle to a minute, under 0.1 pu is an interruption. IEC 61000-4-11 tests — dips and short interruptions for equipment up to 16 A per phase, 61000-4-34 above that, at levels the product standard sets. A compliance mark means the box survived those specified dips against a stated criterion, nothing more.

SEMI F47 requires: sags to 50% for 50 to 200 ms, 70% for 200 to 500 ms and 80% for 500 to 1000 ms. Semiconductor took the shape of the ITIC envelope, deepened the sub-half-second region, and made vendors comply. IT never did.

The events you actually get

Sags, not outages, and mostly short. A fault on one feeder depresses voltage on every feeder off the same bus, so a customer nowhere near it still sees the dip — and it lasts only as long as the protective device takes to clear, typically ten cycles or less. PG&E puts an urban customer at one or two interruptions a year against more than twenty sags.

EPRI's Distribution Power Quality work counted this with SARFI, events per customer per year below a threshold. At distribution feeder locations the median site saw about 31 events a year dip below 80%, but only about 21 fell outside the lower ITI curve. That gap is the duration allowance doing its work.

If most events land inside the envelope and your load still drops, the curve is not the problem. Either something in the room is less tolerant than the curve — a switch, a KVM, a fan controller, an old array shelf — or a protective device did what it was configured to do when nothing needed to happen.

Where the ride-through actually lives

Not in the UPS. At the server it lives in the power supply, in two mechanisms.

First, the active PFC front end boosts rectified input onto a DC bus near 400 V and holds it across a wide input range. A sag to 80% does not lower the bus; the boost stage just draws more current. Sags inside that window are nearly invisible to the load — and a deep sag raises input current sharply, which matters upstream.

Second, holdup time: the bulk capacitor on that bus carrying the downstream converters after input goes away. An energy budget:

½ · C · (V_bus² − V_cutoff²) = P_out · t / η   →   halve the load, double the time

Intel's ATX12V Power Supply Design Guide holds the figure most people quote unknowingly: output regulation maintained through a loss of input at low-line nominal, at maximum continuous load, "for a minimum of 17 ms," with PWR_OK held at least 16 ms after AC loss. Set that against a curve permitting a 20 ms dropout: the envelope is more generous than the floor of what it describes. The heavily loaded box is the one that drops. 80 PLUS is no help — it certifies efficiency at 20%, 50% and 100% of load and is silent on holdup.

Dual cording adds a mechanism and a failure mode. Two supplies OR'd onto a common output share the load; sag one feed hard enough and that supply drops out and the other takes 100% as a step. Fine if the survivor is healthy and has the headroom. But if both feeds trace back to the same upstream source, a sag hits both — redundant cords, not redundant sources. And a failed supply is invisible until the other feed dips, so put per-cord load and supply state in monitoring.

UPS topologies against the curve

IEC 62040-3 classifies UPS by how much of the input the output depends on. VFI is "independent of supply (mains) voltage and frequency variations": the output is synthesized, so input events never reach the curve at the load. VI regulates against continuous under- and overvoltage, but its output "is dependent on a.c. input frequency" — it covers the steady-state band and the shallow end of the sag region, and still goes to battery on a fast deep sag. VFD "shall protect the load from power outage" and nothing else: the far right of the plot, with everything interesting exposed.

Eco mode is worth arithmetic. The load runs on the static bypass, inverter idle, and on an event the UPS must detect, decide, energize the inverter and open the bypass. Schneider Electric's eco-mode white paper puts that sequence at 1 to 16 ms. Two details make the upper end real: a static switch transfers only at a zero crossing or when mains is below inverter voltage, so under reactive load the SCRs may not clear for up to 8 ms, and a static bypass cannot open while power flows to the load, so eco mode cannot protect against sub-cycle overvoltage at all. Sixteen milliseconds against a 20 ms dropout allowance fits, with 4 ms of margin, for equipment that actually meets the curve.

Static switches

A static transfer switch is back-to-back SCRs per phase choosing between two sources; its honest job is giving a single-corded load a second source. Vendors specify quarter-cycle transfer, roughly 4 ms at 60 Hz, comfortably inside the dropout allowance. Two things complicate that.

It must be break-before-make. Vertiv's technical note on transfer algorithms is blunt: "the alternate source cannot be turned on immediately; a break-before-make delay is necessary to avoid cross conduction between sources." You cannot parallel two unsynchronized sources, so there is always a gap.

And the gap is not always a quarter cycle. The common phase delay approach deliberately delays transfer by the phase difference between sources, to keep volt-seconds balanced on a downstream transformer — 90° out of phase is a quarter cycle, 180° is a half cycle, 8.33 ms. It also breaks down when the phases do not collapse together: single-phase loss, a phase-to-phase fault, a drooping source. Sub-cycle transfer is a best case, not a property.

Which is how an STS makes things worse. Put one upstream of a PDU transformer and the transfer can saturate the core; saturation means inrush that overloads the alternate source and trips upstream breakers. You transfer successfully, then lose the load to a coordination problem you created. Two structural points go with that: sources tracing back to a common bus are hardware, not diversity, and the switch is one more series device. And an STS reacts: a brief upset on the preferred feed produces a state change, which across a floor of static-switch PDUs can cascade into overloads and dropped load from an event holdup alone would have covered.

What to measure

Metering at the service entrance tells you what the utility did; metering at the PDU and branch circuit tells you what the load saw. The difference is where your own gear lives: transformers, UPS, static switches, breakers. With only one, every post-event meeting is an argument about which side it came from.

Averages find none of this; a four-cycle sag vanishes into a 15-minute demand average. IEC 61000-4-30 defines what you want instead — U<sub>rms(½)</sub>, the "value of the r.m.s. voltage measured over 1 cycle, commencing at a fundamental zero crossing, and refreshed each half-cycle," for detecting dips, swells and interruptions, and it grades instruments Class A or Class S on measurement performance. Buy Class A at the service entrance if you can, and confirm whatever you buy captures waveforms and timestamps events rather than logging trends.

Having built facilities telemetry from scratch, the thing I would prioritize differently is time. Magnitude and duration are the easy part; getting meters, UPS, PDUs and servers onto one clock to better than a cycle is what turns two logs into one timeline. An IT event you cannot line up against a recorded electrical event will be blamed on the power chain forever, and you will never disprove it.

The curve is a good way to think about tolerance. It is not evidence. Evidence is a timestamped sag with a magnitude, a duration, and a server log beside it.
