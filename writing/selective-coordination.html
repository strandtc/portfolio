Why the wrong breaker trips first

How overcurrent devices are made to open in the right order, how to read a time-current curve, and where the Code actually requires it.

Critical Power8 min read

A power supply shorts in a single 1U server. The branch breaker in the rack PDU should open, and nothing else. Instead the 225 A feeder breaker at the PDU opens, and forty cabinets go dark on the A side. Nobody in the room did anything wrong. The system was protected and it was not coordinated.

Protection and coordination are different jobs. Protection is about clearing a fault fast enough that nothing catches fire and nobody gets hurt. Selective coordination is about which device does the clearing. A system can be perfectly protected and completely uncoordinated, and the failure mode of an uncoordinated system is that a small fault takes out a large piece of the plant.

The NEC defines it in Article 100. The 2014 wording is worth reading slowly:

Localization of an overcurrent condition to restrict outages to the circuit or equipment affected, accomplished by the selection and installation of overcurrent protective devices and their ratings or settings for the full range of available overcurrents, from overload to the maximum available fault current, and for the full range of overcurrent protective device opening times associated with those overcurrents.

Two phrases carry the weight. Full range of available overcurrents - coordinated at 200 A is not coordinated. And selection and installation - a coordinated design that gets built with a different breaker is not coordinated either.

Reading a time-current curve

Everything in coordination is argued on a time-current curve (TCC): a log-log plot with current on the horizontal axis and clearing time on the vertical. Current is normally plotted at one reference voltage so that devices on both sides of a transformer can be drawn on the same sheet.

Each device is drawn as a band, not a line, because manufacturing tolerance is real. For a thermal-magnetic molded case breaker the band is wide. For an electronic trip unit it is narrow.

The rule is simple: the bands must not touch. Read the plot from bottom left to top right. The downstream device's band must sit entirely below and to the left of the upstream device's band, across the whole current range up to the maximum available fault current at that point. Where they overlap, both devices can open for the same fault, and which one actually goes is decided by tolerances and luck.

Two things people miss on their first study. First, curves get plotted only as far right as somebody chose to plot them - if the sheet stops at 20 kA and you have 42 kA available, the study does not cover your system. Second, the transformer inrush point and damage curve belong on the plot too. A feeder breaker set low enough to coordinate downstream may not ride through the magnetizing inrush of the transformer it feeds.

Long-time, short-time, instantaneous

A modern electronic trip unit is described by its functions, which is where the shorthand LSIG comes from.

FunctionWhat it responds toTypical adjustment
L - long-timeOverload. Sustained current above the conductor's ratingPickup (as a fraction of sensor rating) and delay
S - short-timeFault current below the instantaneous thresholdPickup (multiple of long-time pickup) and delay band, with I²t in or out
I - instantaneousHigh fault current, no intentional delayPickup, or off on some units
G - ground faultResidual or zero-sequence currentPickup and delay

Coordination in the long-time region is straightforward - you separate devices by time, and you have seconds to work with. Coordination in the fault region is where systems fail, and the culprit is almost always the instantaneous element.

A thermal-magnetic molded case breaker has a fixed instantaneous and no short-time delay. Once fault current exceeds the instantaneous pickup of both the branch and the feeder breaker, both see the fault, both go instantaneous, and neither one has any intentional delay to lose the race with. There is no setting that fixes this. Coordination in the instantaneous region between two thermal-magnetic breakers is generally not achievable.

The fix is short-time delay upstream: turn the instantaneous off on the feeder or main, and let it wait out the downstream device's clearing time. This is exactly what a drawout insulated-case or power circuit breaker is for. It has a short-time withstand rating, meaning it can hold a fault for the delay period without destroying itself.

There is a catch, and it is the thing that most often breaks an otherwise good study. Molded-case and insulated-case breakers with electronic trip units carry an instantaneous override - a fixed, non-defeatable element that protects the breaker from currents beyond what its contacts can hold. Turning the adjustable instantaneous off does not disable it. Vendors commonly place the override in the range of roughly eight to twelve times the breaker rating. Above the override, short-time delay stops mattering and you are back to two devices racing. Whether that matters depends on whether the available fault current at that point exceeds the override.

Fuses behave differently and, for coordination, more agreeably. Current-limiting fuses of the same family coordinate on an ampere-rating ratio rather than a curve overlap, because the selectivity condition is that the load-side fuse's total clearing energy stays below the line-side fuse's melting energy. Manufacturers publish selectivity ratio guides; for the common current-limiting families a 2:1 ratio is the usual requirement, valid at all overcurrents and all opening times. That makes the study a table lookup instead of a plot. The trade is everything else about fuses: single-phasing, replacement stock, and a technician standing in front of an energized enclosure with a fuse puller.

Where coordination and protection pull against each other

Every second of short-time delay you add upstream to gain selectivity is a second the fault burns. Incident energy is roughly proportional to arcing current times duration, so the setting that saves the site from a cascading outage also raises the arc flash hazard at that piece of gear.

This is a genuine engineering trade, not a thing to be solved by picking a side. You size the delay to the minimum that achieves selectivity, you use zone selective interlocking where you can, and you accept the label the calculation gives you. What you must not do is set short-time delay generously to make a study close and then forget to redo the incident energy analysis. NFPA 70E requires the incident energy analysis to be updated when changes to the electrical distribution system could affect the result, and reviewed at intervals not exceeding five years. A breaker setting change is such a change.

Ground fault protection has its own version of the problem. NEC 230.95 requires ground-fault protection of equipment on solidly grounded wye services over 150 V to ground and not exceeding 1000 V phase-to-phase, for each service disconnect rated 1000 A or more, with a maximum pickup setting of 1200 A and a maximum time delay of one second for ground faults of 3000 A or more. One GFP element at the service with nothing downstream cannot be selective with anything. A ground fault on any branch trips the service. If you want selectivity on ground faults you need ground fault sensing at more than one level, and delay bands between them, within that one-second ceiling.

Zone selective interlocking

Zone selective interlocking (ZSI) is the way out of the delay-versus-energy trade for the specific case of a fault inside the upstream device's own zone.

The trip units are wired together with a small interlock pair. When a downstream breaker sees a fault above its pickup, it sends a restraining signal upstream. The upstream breaker, on receiving restraint, uses its programmed short-time delay - it waits, because it knows a device closer to the fault is handling it. If the upstream breaker sees fault current and receives no restraining signal, the fault must be between it and the next device down, so it trips with minimal delay instead.

The result is selectivity when you need it and fast clearing when you do not. It costs a pair of conductors between trip units, correct commissioning, and the discipline to actually test it. An interlock wire that was never landed looks exactly like a working system until the day the upstream breaker sits there for its full delay on a fault in its own bus.

Where the Code requires it

Selective coordination is required in a short and specific list of places, and the IT load is not on it.

NEC sectionSystem
620.62Elevators, escalators and similar, where more than one drive is on a single feeder
700.32Emergency systems
701.32Legally required standby systems
708.54Critical operations power systems (COPS)
517Health care essential electrical systems

The 2023 NEC tightened 700.32: emergency system overcurrent devices must be selectively coordinated with supply-side and load-side devices, and selective coordination must be re-evaluated when devices are replaced or when the system is modified. The long-standing exception still stands - coordination is not required between two devices in series if no loads are connected in parallel with the downstream device.

For a commercial data center, that usually catches the life safety branch, the fire pump if there is one, and the elevators. Nothing in the Code requires you to coordinate the UPS output distribution. You do that because losing a whole PDU to a bad power supply is a business event and losing a branch circuit is not.

What to check

The study is not the deliverable. The settings in the field are the deliverable, and they drift.

  • Every breaker setting change and every breaker replacement invalidates the study until somebody re-checks it. Write that into the change process, not into a binder.
  • Record the as-set values on a label inside the cubicle door, dated. Trip units get replaced, batteries die, and settings default.
  • Verify the maximum available fault current the study assumed against what the utility gives you today. Substation upgrades raise it, and nobody calls to tell you.
  • Confirm the instantaneous override for every electronic-trip breaker you are relying on for short-time selectivity, and confirm it sits above the available fault current at that point.
  • If you have ZSI, prove the interlock wiring during commissioning with an actual restrained and unrestrained test, and prove it again after any switchgear work.
