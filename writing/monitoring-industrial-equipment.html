Monitoring the equipment that keeps the building alive

The protocols facilities gear actually speaks, how to get it into one system, and how to build alerting people will still read in a year.

Systems8 min read

Server monitoring is a solved problem with a large ecosystem. Facilities monitoring is not, because the equipment predates the ecosystem, comes from a dozen vendors with a dozen assumptions, and in several cases speaks a protocol invented for factory floors in the 1970s. Building telemetry for a data center means learning half a dozen of these and then doing the hard part, which is deciding what deserves to wake someone up.

I built facilities telemetry from scratch — UPS, cooling, environmental, physical security, with alerting we wrote ourselves — because at the time there was nothing you could buy that covered all of it. There still isn't, really. What has changed is that the integration work is now mostly a matter of gateways and patience.

What is actually in the building

Before you pick tools, inventory the interfaces. In a typical facility you will find most of these:

SourceUsually speaks
UPSSNMP via a network management card; RS-232 or Modbus natively
PDUs, rack and floor-mountSNMP, increasingly a REST API too
Switchgear, breakers, metersModbus RTU or TCP
ATS and generator controllersModbus, plus dry contacts for the important states
CRAC and CRAH unitsModbus or BACnet, depending on the vendor and the year
Chillers, BMS, air handlersBACnet
Leak detection, smoke, securityDry contacts, or a proprietary panel with a contact interface
Temperature and humidity1-Wire, I2C, Modbus, or a networked probe
Generic transducers4–20 mA or 0–10 V into an analog input module

Nothing in that list speaks the same language as anything else in it. The job is to normalize it without building something so bespoke that only you can maintain it.

SNMP cards, and their limits

Most UPSs and nearly all managed PDUs take a network management card that speaks SNMP. There is even a standard MIB: RFC 1628, the UPS Management Information Base, published in 1994 with contributions from most of the UPS vendors of the day. It gives you battery status, input and output measurements, alarms and a set of traps.

Support is real but partial. Vendors implement some of RFC 1628 and then put everything interesting — per-phase detail, battery age and test results, module-level state in a modular UPS — in their enterprise MIB. Plan to poll both: RFC 1628 for the things you want to alert on uniformly across a mixed fleet, the vendor MIB for the diagnostics.

Two operational cautions. These cards are small embedded computers with modest processors and other responsibilities; walking the entire tree every thirty seconds is a good way to make one unresponsive, and I have seen them reboot under polling load. Ask for the specific OIDs you need, at an interval measured in tens of seconds, and back off on timeouts.

And the management card is not the UPS. When the card locks up, the UPS is fine and your monitoring is blind — which is exactly the failure mode where you most want the truth. Anything genuinely critical should also come out on a dry contact wired to something else.

Modbus

Modbus is the lingua franca of electrical and mechanical gear. Modbus RTU runs over a serial line, usually RS-485 multidrop, with a device address and a CRC on every frame. Modbus TCP wraps the same messages in a short header on TCP port 502, the IANA-assigned port. Same register model, same function codes.

The model is four spaces of addresses: coils and discrete inputs for single bits, input registers and holding registers for 16-bit words. You read holding registers with function code 3 and write them with 6 for one register or 16 for several.

What makes Modbus painful is what it does not carry. A register is sixteen bits and nothing else. It does not tell you whether that word is signed, whether it is half of a 32-bit float, what the scaling factor is, or which order the words go in. All of that lives in the vendor's register map, which is a PDF, and every vendor has chosen differently. A 32-bit value split across two registers can be big-endian, little-endian, or word-swapped, and you find out which by reading a value you can verify independently.

Practical rules for Modbus:

  • Get the register map document for the exact firmware revision. Not the family, the revision.
  • Verify every scaled value against a physical reading — the display on the front of the device,

or a clamp meter — before you trust a graph built on it.

  • On RS-485, respect the topology. Daisy chain, not a star. Termination at both physical ends.

Common ground reference. Loading rules limit a segment to a modest number of nodes, commonly 32 without repeaters.

  • One master per serial segment. Two pollers on one bus produce intermittent garbage that will

take you a week to diagnose.

  • There is no authentication anywhere in Modbus. Anything speaking Modbus TCP belongs on an

isolated network with no route to anything else.

BACnet

BACnet is ANSI/ASHRAE Standard 135, also published as ISO 16484-5, and it is what the mechanical side of the building runs on. Compared to Modbus it is genuinely well designed: instead of raw registers it has typed objects with named properties. An analog input object has a Present_Value, a Units property, a Description you can read, and an Out_Of_Service flag that tells you whether the point is live. You can browse a device and discover what it has without a PDF.

The transports are BACnet/IP, defined in Annex J of the standard, and BACnet MS/TP over RS-485 for field-level devices. BACnet/IP uses UDP — port 47808, 0xBAC0, is the default, and compliant devices must support a configurable port covering at least 47808–47823 and 49152–65535.

The thing that surprises IP people is that BACnet/IP relies on broadcasts for discovery, and broadcasts do not cross a router. The answer is a BACnet Broadcast Management Device: a BBMD on each subnet holds a broadcast distribution table listing the other BBMDs, wraps local broadcasts as directed messages, and re-broadcasts them at the far end. A device elsewhere can instead register as a foreign device with a BBMD, which adds it to a foreign device table and forwards broadcasts to it. If your BACnet discovery works on one subnet and silently finds nothing across a router, this is why, every time.

BACnet also supports change of value subscriptions. Use COV for anything that changes rarely and matters immediately, and poll for anything you want a regular time series of.

Contacts, current loops and cheap sensors

Not everything needs a protocol.

A dry contact is a relay closure with no voltage of its own. Generators, ATS controllers, leak detection cable, door contacts and fire panels all offer them, and they are the most reliable signal in the building because there is nothing to crash. You supply the sense voltage and read the state. Learn the difference between form A (normally open), form B (normally closed) and form C (changeover), and prefer normally closed for anything safety-relevant — a cut wire then reads as an alarm rather than as silence. Better still, use supervised inputs with an end-of-line resistor, which lets you distinguish open, closed and broken.

For analog values, 4–20 mA current loops are worth the extra cost over 0–10 V almost every time. Current is the same everywhere in a series loop, so wire resistance does not change the reading, and a loop runs reliably over distances where a voltage signal has already degraded. The 4 mA "live zero" is the real advantage: zero means the loop is broken, 4 mA means the sensor is alive and reading its minimum. With 0–10 V, zero volts is indistinguishable from a dead transmitter. Loop-powered two-wire transmitters also carry power and signal on the same pair. Keep 0–10 V for short runs inside a panel where the noise environment is benign.

For temperature and humidity, cheap digital sensors do fine. A DS18B20 on 1-Wire covers −55 to +125 °C, is accurate to about ±0.5 °C between −10 and +85 °C, carries a unique 64-bit ID so you can address many on one bus, and can run parasitically off the data line — more than sufficient for a rack inlet. A Sensirion SHT3x on I²C gives roughly ±2 % RH and ±0.2 °C typical.

The caveat is bus length. 1-Wire will run tens of meters if you keep the topology sane — linear, not a star, with proper pull-up. I²C was designed for chip-to-chip communication on a single board and does not travel; if you want an I²C sensor at the far end of a room, put a small microcontroller next to it and speak something else over the distance.

Gateways, and where to put the intelligence

You will not get everything into one protocol, so decide deliberately where the translation happens. Protocol gateways — Modbus-to-SNMP, BACnet-to-Modbus, almost any combination — are inexpensive DIN-rail devices that do exactly one job.

Two design rules. Put the gateway close to the field devices, so the fragile serial wiring is short and the long run is Ethernet. And keep the gateway dumb: it translates, it does not decide. Logic that lives in a gateway configuration screen is logic nobody will find in two years. Alarm thresholds, hysteresis and suppression belong in your monitoring platform, in version control, where they can be reviewed.

Normalize on arrival. Every point gets a consistent name, a unit, a location and a device identity, applied at ingest, so a temperature from a BACnet CRAH and a temperature from a 1-Wire probe are the same kind of thing by the time anyone queries them.

Alerting people will not learn to ignore

The failure mode of facilities monitoring is not missing data. It is 300 nuisance alarms a week until the on-call engineer builds a mail rule, and then the one that mattered goes into the same folder.

Rob Ewaschuk's writing on alerting, from his time on-call at Google, still frames this best: alert on symptoms rather than causes, and hold every page to the standard that it must be actionable, urgent, and require human intelligence rather than a scriptable response. If the response is mechanical, automate it. If it can wait until morning, it is a ticket. Ewaschuk's other point is the uncomfortable one — over-monitoring is harder to fix than under-monitoring, because deleting an alert feels like accepting risk while adding one feels like diligence.

Translated into a mechanical room:

  • Alarm on the condition that hurts, not on every sensor that participates in it. "Cold aisle

inlet above threshold in row C" is one alarm. Six individual probes crossing a line is six alarms describing one event.

  • Use hysteresis and a time delay on everything analog. A threshold with no deadband will chatter

around the setpoint all night.

  • Suppress the dependents. When utility power fails, you do not need alarms from the generator

starting, the ATS transferring, the UPS discharging and every CRAC restarting. You need one event that says the site is on generator, with the rest as detail.

  • Distinguish urgency in the routing, not in the alert text. Page, ticket, and log are three

different destinations, and every alert gets assigned to one on the day it is written.

  • Every alert carries a link to what to do about it, and the name of the person who owns it.
  • Review what fired every month. Anything that fired and required no action gets fixed or deleted.

The measure of a facilities monitoring system is not how many points it collects. It is whether, at three in the morning, the person who gets the call believes it.
