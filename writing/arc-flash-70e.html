Reading an arc flash label and knowing what is behind it

What an incident energy calculation computes, how the two NFPA 70E methods differ, why studies go stale, and how to cut energy rather than exposure.

Critical Power8 min read

An arc flash is a fault that goes through the air instead of through copper. Ionized air conducts, the arc sustains itself, and the copper at each end vaporizes and expands enormously as it does. What comes out of the enclosure is thermal radiation, a pressure wave, molten metal, and vaporized copper condensing on whatever it hits.

The label on the front of the gear quantifies one part of that - the thermal part - at one distance, for one set of assumptions. Understanding those assumptions is the difference between a label that protects someone and a label that gives them false confidence.

What the calculation computes

Incident energy is the thermal energy delivered to a surface at a stated distance from the arc, in calories per square centimeter. That is it. Not pressure, not shrapnel, not sound, not the toxicity of vaporized insulation.

IEEE 1584 is the model, and the 2018 edition is a significant rework of the 2002 one. Inputs are bolted fault current available at that point, system voltage, electrode gap and configuration, enclosure dimensions, working distance, grounding arrangement, and - the whole ballgame - how long the upstream protective device takes to clear the arcing current.

The 2018 electrode configurations matter more than they sound. The model now distinguishes vertical electrodes in a box (VCB), vertical electrodes terminating in an insulating barrier in a box (VCBB), and horizontal electrodes in a box (HCB), plus open-air equivalents. Where the arc plasma gets directed changes how much energy reaches a person in front of the opening: at typical working distances and all else equal, HCB is worse than VCBB, which is worse than VCB. The 2018 edition also made the valid electrode gap range voltage-dependent - 0.25 to 3 inches for 208 to 600 V, 0.75 to 10 inches for 601 V to 15 kV - and added a correction for enclosure size.

An old study run on the 2002 model and a new one run on 2018 will not agree, and the new one is often higher in places you were comfortable with.

Time dominates, counterintuitively

Incident energy scales with how long the arc burns. A device clearing in three cycles and one clearing in thirty, on the same fault, differ by a factor of ten in delivered energy.

The consequence that surprises people: reducing available fault current can increase incident energy. A smaller arcing current may fall below the upstream breaker's instantaneous pickup, so instead of clearing in a couple of cycles the breaker rides its short-time or long-time delay curve and takes a second or more. Less current, much more time, more energy at the worker.

That is why IEEE 1584 requires evaluating both the calculated arcing current and a reduced arcing current and taking the worse result - and why the label on a small panel at the end of a long feeder is sometimes worse than the label on the switchgear.

cal/cm² and the boundaries

1.2 cal/cm² is the number the industry is organized around. NFPA 70E defines the arc flash boundary as the distance at which incident energy equals 1.2 cal/cm² (5.0 J/cm²), which is the onset of a second-degree burn on bare skin.

It is a burn threshold, not a safe distance. Standing just outside the boundary in a t-shirt means you are barely not getting a second-degree burn from the thermal component, while still being inside the pressure wave and the range of ejected material.

Separately, and often confused with it, are the shock boundaries: limited approach and restricted approach. Those are functions of system voltage and have nothing to do with incident energy. A 480 V panel and a 13.8 kV cubicle can have similar incident energy and completely different shock boundaries. Two hazards, two sets of distances.

The two methods, which you must not mix

NFPA 70E gives two ways to select PPE, and using both at once is a documented mistake.

Incident energy analysis. Calculate the incident energy at the working distance, then select PPE with an arc rating at or above it. Table 130.5(G) tells you the clothing and equipment that goes with the calculated value. This is what an engineering study produces and it is what any facility of any size should have.

PPE category method. Use the task tables - 130.7(C)(15)(a) and (b) - which cover specific equipment types operating within stated parameters of fault current and clearing time. If your equipment fits those parameters, the table gives a category and Table 130.7(C)(15)(c) says what the category requires. If it does not fit, the tables do not apply and you have to calculate.

There is no category above 4. Above 40 cal/cm² you have left the table behind, and the honest answer is that you are not doing that work energized.

Do not put both a calculated incident energy and a PPE category on the same label. 130.5(H) explicitly allows one or the other, not both, precisely because the two mean different things and a reader will apply whichever is more convenient.

Labels

Two different documents drive two different labels and they get conflated constantly.

NFPA 70E 130.5(H) applies to equipment likely to be examined, adjusted, serviced or maintained while energized, in other than dwelling units. The label needs nominal system voltage, arc flash boundary, and at least one of: available incident energy with its working distance, or the arc flash PPE category (one or the other, not both); the minimum arc rating of clothing; or a site-specific level of PPE. The owner is responsible for the label.

NEC 110.16(B) applies to service equipment rated 1200 A or more in other than dwelling units, and requires a permanent field- or factory-applied label carrying nominal system voltage, available fault current at the service overcurrent devices, the clearing time of those devices at that fault current, and the date the label was applied. There is an exception for equipment labeled per acceptable industry practice such as NFPA 70E.

They overlap but they are not the same information, and the NEC one carries a date for a reason.

Why studies go stale

70E requires the arc flash risk assessment to be reviewed when a major modification or renovation occurs and at intervals not exceeding five years. Five years is a ceiling, not a schedule, and here is what changes faster than that:

The utility. They replace a transformer with a lower-impedance unit or reconductor a feeder, and available fault current at your service goes up. Nobody tells you. Ask for it in writing periodically; it is the input that most often invalidates a study.

Generation. Adding a generator, or a second one to a paralleling bus, changes fault current on the emergency source. Sites frequently model utility source and never generator source, so the study is silent about exactly the condition under which people are most likely to be in the gear.

Settings. Somebody adjusts a relay or trip unit during a coordination fix, or turns the instantaneous off to stop nuisance trips, and the clearing time in the study is no longer the clearing time in the field. Every settings change is an arc flash change.

Breakers that no longer do what their curve says. The study assumes published time-current characteristics. A twenty-year-old breaker never exercised or primary-injection tested may not clear in the time the curve promises. That does not make the label conservative, it makes it wrong. An arc flash program without a breaker testing program is half a program.

Drift. Equipment swapped for a different frame, temporary feeds that became permanent, panel schedules that no longer match reality. Studies model documented systems, and documentation decays.

Reducing the energy rather than the exposure

Because incident energy tracks clearing time, the highest-leverage fix is almost always to make the upstream device faster while someone is standing in front of the gear.

NEC 240.87 requires arc energy reduction where a circuit breaker's continuous current rating or trip setting is 1200 A or more, and lists the acceptable means: zone-selective interlocking, differential relaying, energy-reducing maintenance switching with a local status indicator, an energy-reducing active arc flash mitigation system, an instantaneous trip setting or an instantaneous override lower than the available arcing current, or an approved equivalent. It also requires documentation of where these devices are and how to engage them, plus performance testing by a qualified person at installation. 240.67 does the equivalent for fuses.

The energy-reducing maintenance switch is the one worth understanding. It temporarily reconfigures the breaker's protection to a fast, low instantaneous setting, deliberately sacrificing selective coordination in exchange for speed, for as long as a person is exposed. Throw it, do the work, throw it back. It routinely takes a 30 cal/cm² label down to something in category 2 territory.

Two things make it useless. If it sits inside the arc flash boundary of the equipment it protects, engaging it requires the exposure it exists to prevent - put it remote. And if the local status indicator does not work, or nobody was trained to use it, it is an expensive switch nobody throws.

Zone-selective interlocking is the more elegant version: downstream devices signal upstream ones during a fault, so the closest device clears fast without the upstream one waiting out a coordination delay. Speed and selectivity at once, and worth specifying on new switchgear. Remote racking attacks the problem from the other end - if nobody is in front of the gear, the energy at the gear does not matter.

The thing people get wrong

They treat the study as a PPE purchasing exercise. Consultant delivers a report, facilities buys suits to match the highest number, labels go on doors, box ticked.

The most useful output of an arc flash study is not the PPE list. It is the map of where incident energy is high and why - which is almost always that a large device upstream is slow at that fault current. That is a protection coordination finding. Fix the coordination, add energy reduction where 240.87 applies, and most of the high-cal locations come down permanently, for everyone who ever works there. PPE only protects the person wearing it correctly on that day.

The hierarchy still holds. PPE is the last control. The first is not being there: de-energize, verify absence of voltage, apply locks. Everything after that point in 70E is what to do when that was genuinely not possible, which is a much smaller set of situations than most facilities behave as though it is.
