Telling imposter syndrome apart from an actual skill gap

Why the feeling targets people who keep changing domains, how to distinguish it from a real gap, and what reduces it besides reassurance.

Practice7 min read

The people who feel most like frauds are usually the ones who keep taking on work they have not done before. That is not a coincidence and it is not a disorder. If your career involves moving between domains, you will spend a large fraction of it as the least experienced person in the room about the thing you are currently doing.

I have gone from Linux systems to critical power to networks to compliance programs and, recently, to embedded work — carrier board design, Yocto, Qt, CAN bus. In each of those there were people with a decade in the field who knew things I did not. The first data center I built, I had not built a data center. That is what "first" means. It is not a special condition requiring an explanation.

Why it targets the people it targets

Stay in one discipline for twenty years and you accumulate an unbroken record of knowing what you are doing. Confidence in that situation is mostly pattern recognition — you have seen this before, and you have seen the exceptions.

Move between disciplines and the counter resets. For the first stretch in a new field you are, accurately, worse than the specialists around you. Meanwhile you are being treated according to your previous record, as the person who built the thing, while privately aware that you have never specified this particular piece of equipment before. That gap between how you are addressed and what you currently know is the entire sensation. It is not a distortion. It is a real gap. It just is not the gap people think it is, which is about belonging rather than about a subject.

There is a second mechanism and it is worse for competent people: the more you learn about a field, the better your inventory of what you do not know. Someone six months in has a short list of unknowns because they cannot see most of them yet. Someone six years in has a long one. Both are right about the length of their own list. Only one of them should feel comforted by it.

The feeling and the gap are different things

This is the distinction that makes the topic useful rather than therapeutic.

Imposter syndrome is the belief that you will be exposed as not belonging, held despite evidence that you do. A skill gap is: there is a specific thing you do not know and the work requires it. At two in the morning they feel identical, and they call for opposite responses.

QuestionThe feelingA real gap
Can you name it precisely?No, it is about you in generalYes, it has a noun
Does evidence change it?NoYes
Does it survive a success?Yes, within about an hourNo
Correct responseShip something, record it, askLearn it, or say you cannot yet

The naming test does most of the work. "I do not know how a selective coordination study is performed" is a gap: it has a subject, a method, and somebody you could go ask. "I do not belong in this meeting" is a feeling, and there is nothing in it to act on. Gaps have nouns. Feelings have adjectives about you.

The second test is nearly as good. Would a competent outsider agree? If someone reviewed two years of your output — what shipped, what is still running, which audits passed — would they conclude you were unqualified? If not, then the disagreement is between you and the record, not between you and reality. You can go and ask this out loud, which most people never do because they are afraid of the answer, and the answer is almost always duller and more useful than the fear.

What actually reduces it

Reassurance does not. Being told you are good buys about ninety minutes of relief and is then discounted as politeness, which it frequently is. Three things work.

Shipping. Not praise — building something that exists afterward and keeps running when you are not looking at it. A commissioned building. A network carrying production traffic. A platform other people depend on. The strongest available counter to "I do not belong here" is an object. Arguments about your competence are unwinnable; artifacts are not arguable.

Keeping a record. Write down what you did, when, and how it came out. Memory is unreliable and biased against you specifically: you will retain the outage in detail and lose the eighteen quiet months around it. A written list of delivered work is something you can read on a bad day, and it is also the raw material for the promotion case or the resume you will eventually need, so the effort is not spent purely on your feelings. Once a month is enough. What shipped, what broke, what changed.

Learning to say "I don't know yet." This is the largest of the three and the least obvious. The specific fear inside imposter syndrome is being found out. Saying "I don't know" deliberately, out loud, on your own schedule, removes that fear by doing the thing yourself, in a form that reads as competence instead of exposure. Nobody has ever thought less of an engineer who said "I have not done that — give me until Thursday and I will have an answer." They think a great deal less of the one who guessed and turned out to be wrong in a design review, with the drawings already at the contractor.

The "yet" is doing real work in that sentence. "I don't know" describes a permanent condition. "I don't know yet" describes a schedule.

The confident people in the room

Worth saying plainly, because it gets left out of most writing on this: confidence is mostly temperament and repetition, not knowledge. In any technical review there is somebody speaking with total certainty about a system they have never operated. They are not lying. They feel certain, because they do not yet know enough to see where the exceptions are.

The ones who hedge — "it depends on the available fault current," "I would want to see the coordination study before I answer that" — are frequently the people who have already been caught by the exception. In operations, hedging is often a symptom of experience rather than a shortage of it.

The habit worth building is to judge calibration rather than volume. Does this person's confidence track their accuracy? Do they say "I'm sure" and turn out to be right, and "I'm not sure" and turn out to be the one who found the real problem? Then apply the same test to yourself, which is where it actually pays. Keep the record long enough and you can check whether your own certainty predicts anything at all. Most people find theirs is badly calibrated in both directions, and unlike a feeling, calibration is something you can practice.

When the gap is real

Sometimes you are underqualified. In work that keeps changing this happens regularly and it is not a crisis.

Say it specifically rather than generally. "I have never done X. I need three weeks and a review from someone who has" is a professional statement that gives your manager something to work with. "I'm not sure I'm the right person for this" is not information; it hands the problem back unsolved.

Then get the reviewer, because every serious technical discipline already has this built in. The engineer of record stamps the drawings. The commissioning agent tests what the contractor actually installed. The external auditor checks the controls rather than taking your word for them. None of that exists because practitioners lack confidence. It exists because independent review catches things, reliably, and everyone in the field knows it. Asking for it on your first attempt at something is using the profession as designed.

And take the smaller version first where the work allows it. Crawl, walk, run applies to people as much as to products — run the maintenance window on the least critical system before you run it on the busiest one. Earning the next stage is not timidity. It is how you end up qualified rather than merely promoted.

Start the record this month. What you finished, what failed, what you learned. Ten years of that will answer the question of whether you belong more honestly than any feeling you are going to have about it, and unlike the feeling, you can go back and check it.
